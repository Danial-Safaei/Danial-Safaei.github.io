PhD researcher · Safe AI for self-driving cars

Danial Safaei

Self-driving cars practise in computer-made worlds before they drive on real roads. I check that they learn the right lessons there.

Self-driving cars are tested in simulators. I build checks that show whether the car’s AI passes those tests for the right reasons.

Car makers test their AI in simulation. I build tools that show when it passes a simulated test for the wrong reason, so the results can be trusted.

The idea

A test can pass for the wrong reason

An AI can give the right answer in a simulator while looking at the wrong things. My work makes that visible and measurable.

Showing the AnyoneStudentManagerEngineerML engineerResearcherSpecialist version. Change

Before a self-driving car drives on real roads, it practises in a computer-made world, a bit like a very realistic video game. A test there checks whether the car makes the right choice, such as stopping for a person.

But a car can make the right choice for the wrong reason. Maybe it stopped because it noticed a lamp post, not the person. On a real street, that could go badly wrong.

My work checks what the car was actually looking at when it made its choice, in the game world and in the real world. If it looks at the same things in both, we can trust the game’s test results more.

Companies test self-driving software in simulators because it is safer, faster and cheaper than driving millions of real miles. Usually a test only checks the answer: did the software find the road, the lane, the pedestrian?

I add a second check: did it use the same clues in the simulator as on a real road? To find the clues, I use explainable AI, which highlights the parts of an image that decided the answer. Then I compare those highlights on a real photo and on its simulated copy.

If the clues are different, a test passed in the simulator may tell us little about the real world.

Car makers rely on simulation to test camera-based AI, because real-world driving alone cannot cover enough situations. The risk is a test that passes for the wrong reason: the AI gives the right output in simulation while relying on different visual cues than it would on a real road.

Standard checks compare outputs or how realistic the images look, so they cannot see this. I developed two metrics, DFF and C-DFF, that compare the evidence behind each decision on matched real and simulated images. They show when simulation results can be trusted, and which parts of a simulated scene need fixing.

The work is published at IEEE IV 2026 and IEEE ITSC 2026, with Siemens Digital Industries Software as industrial partner.

Take a real camera frame and its synthetic twin: the same scene geometry, rendered by a simulator or a generative model. Run both through the same frozen perception model, so any difference comes from the data and not the model.

Counterfactual explanations then find the image regions whose change would flip each output: the decisive evidence. DFF compares the two evidence maps and gives one agreement score per pair. C-DFF maps the evidence onto scene concepts such as road, cars, poles, buildings and sky, and reports which concepts gained or lost importance in simulation.

Evaluated on 2,126 matched KITTI and Virtual KITTI 2 pairs with three frozen models: a PilotNet-style steering network and the drivable-area and lane-line outputs of YOLOP.

Output agreement and image similarity (SSIM, LPIPS) do not tell you whether a network used the same features. DFF estimates decisive features with counterfactual explanations on a frozen system under test, separately for the real input and its synthetic counterpart, and scores their agreement. C-DFF projects that evidence into a shared concept space using off-the-shelf semantic segmentation and compares the concept-importance vectors.

On KITTI and Virtual KITTI 2, the rank correlation between output agreement and evidence agreement was weak (|ρ| ≤ 0.21). Tuning an image generator for output agreement alone cut the output loss by 74% while evidence divergence rose by 16%: in that experiment, optimising the usual metric widened the evidence gap.

The research question is mechanism parity: does a frozen system under test reach its output through the same decisive evidence on paired real and synthetic inputs? DFF answers at the level of evidence maps and C-DFF at the level of semantic concepts. Both are validation diagnostics, not safety certificates.

The full-length preprint unifies the two on a deterministic, exact-geometry KITTI to Virtual KITTI 2 benchmark with ten retrained ControlNet generators and three frozen systems. Output and appearance fidelity are at most weakly and inconsistently rank-correlated with decisive-mechanism distance, and a constructed positive control shows that the estimator detects a planted mechanism that output agreement misses. Claims are limited to the declared benchmark, generators and systems.

Given a frozen SUT f and a paired input (xr, xs) with exact geometric correspondence, DFF compares decisive-feature maps estimated by counterfactual explanation for each input. C-DFF pools these maps over a shared segmentation into concept-importance vectors and compares them, giving a scalar parity score and a signed concept-shift profile.

Uncertainty is reported with pair-clustered bootstrap intervals, alongside permutation tests, threshold-sensitivity analysis and held-out replication. Results so far: weak output-to-evidence rank correlation (|ρ| ≤ 0.21; 2,126 pairs; steering, YOLOP drivable-area and YOLOP lane-line SUTs); pass rates of 77%, 97% and 67% for those three SUTs at SUT-specific thresholds; and a planted-mechanism positive control that the estimator detects where output agreement is blind.

Key words, in plain English
Simulation
A computer-made version of the world, used to test a car’s software safely.
Synthetic image (twin)
A computer-made picture of the same scene as a real photo.
Frozen model
An AI whose settings are locked, so it behaves the same way in every test.
Explainable AI
Methods that show which parts of the input an AI relied on.
Counterfactual explanation
“What is the smallest change to the image that would change the AI’s answer?” Those parts are the decisive evidence.
Mechanism parity
The AI reaches its answer the same way in simulation as on real roads.

How it works

Four steps, from two pictures to a verdict

The same pipeline sits behind both papers. The words change with your reading level; the steps do not.

  1. STEP 1

    Pair

    Take a real photo and its simulated copy of the same scene.

    A real frame and its exact-geometry synthetic twin, from a simulator or a generative model.

  2. STEP 2

    Freeze

    Show both pictures to the same AI, without changing it.

    One frozen system under test; weights fixed, so differences come from the data.

  3. STEP 3

    Explain

    Highlight the parts of each picture that decided the AI’s answer.

    Counterfactual explanations give a decisive-evidence map for each input.

  4. STEP 4

    Compare

    Compare the highlights. Do they point at the same things?

    DFF scores evidence agreement; C-DFF reports the shift per scene concept.

Same evidence

The simulated result can count as evidence about the real road.

Different evidence

Do not trust that result yet. C-DFF says which parts of the simulated scene to fix.

Shows which things in the scene, such as cars, poles or buildings, the AI paid more or less attention to in the simulation.

Turns the evidence check into a list of scene concepts that matter more or less in simulation, so simulator teams know what to improve.

Mechanism parity The full study, uniting DFF and C-DFF

A bigger study with ten different image generators. It showed that a realistic-looking image does not guarantee that the AI uses the same clues.

Brings DFF and C-DFF together and tests them at scale: ten image generators and three frozen AI systems. Image realism and output agreement did not reliably predict whether the AI used the same evidence.

A deterministic exact-geometry KITTI to Virtual KITTI 2 benchmark with bootstrap uncertainty and threshold-sensitivity analysis. Output and appearance fidelity are at most weakly and inconsistently rank-correlated with decisive-mechanism distance.

Positive control. A deliberately planted change in the AI’s evidence was caught by the evidence check and missed by output agreement.

About me

Background and experience

I am a PhD researcher in the Safe Autonomy Research Group at WMG, University of Warwick, working with Siemens Digital Industries Software. My research asks when simulation can be trusted as evidence that the AI in a self-driving car is safe.

Before Warwick, I worked on deep learning, reinforcement learning and computer vision at the University of Tehran. I care about evaluation that is honest about its limits: clear claims, real uncertainty, and code that others can run.
