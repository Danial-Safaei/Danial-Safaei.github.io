In August, an official at the Danish embassy telephoned me without warning and began asking questions. Why did I want to visit Denmark. Which places, specifically. Why, in particular, did I want to see the gardens around Rosenborg Castle. Did I have any final comment.

The last question told me what kind of call it was.

He had the file open in front of him. The gardens he was asking about were on page thirty-eight of the application I had lodged a month earlier — a day-by-day itinerary naming ten places in Copenhagen, with flight numbers, an address, and a hostel I had already paid for. I answered him. I told him I would spend three days in Copenhagen and then travel to Naples to present a paper at the IEEE International Conference on Intelligent Transportation Systems.

Ten days later the refusal arrived, citing a single ground out of seventeen: justification for the purpose and conditions of the intended stay was not provided. The letter explained, not unkindly, that an applicant should be able to name the sights he wishes to see.

I had named them. In writing, a month earlier, in the document the officer was reading from while he asked.

Every other box on the form was left blank. They did not doubt my funds, my documents, my insurance, or that I would return home. A file of forty-nine pages was decided on a judgement about how convincingly I discussed a garden.

This was my second refusal in three months. Spain had declined me in June. And before either, there was Detroit — my first paper, accepted at IEEE Intelligent Vehicles, in a country whose restrictions on Iranian nationals meant there was never any realistic prospect of my attending. Three papers in two years. Two flagship conferences. No rooms entered.

I want to be precise about what I am and am not claiming. I am not alleging bad faith. I have no evidence that anyone decided anything on the basis of my nationality, and the refusal letters do not say so — the boxes that would indicate a security or public-policy concern were left conspicuously blank. The officials who assessed me applied their criteria and reached a conclusion that was defensible within them.

That is rather the point. Nothing malfunctioned. The design is the problem.

The financial arithmetic is not trivial for a doctoral student. Flights, accommodation, the visa fee, agency service charges — about £490 for this application alone, none of it recoverable. The hostel bed I will never sleep in was non-refundable from the moment I booked it. The visa fee is retained whatever the outcome; that is the standard everywhere.

Scaled up, that stops looking like an administrative footnote. Rejected Schengen applications generated €157 million in non-refundable fees in 2025 alone, and €432 million across the three years to 2025 — money paid by people who received nothing whatever in return. Marta Foresti of the LAGO Collective calls these reverse remittances: capital flowing from poor countries to rich ones, one refusal at a time. Africa and Asia account for roughly 90 per cent of it.

The academic figures are equally uncomfortable. Studies of international conferences have found that around 40 per cent of Asian and 34 per cent of African researchers encounter visa obstacles, against 9 per cent of their Western colleagues. Refusal rates for short-term academic visas exceed 30 per cent. These are not anecdotes. They are the ordinary operating conditions of a career.

What is lost when a researcher cannot reach a conference is not the trip. Conferences are not tourism with slides. They are where a field decides what it thinks — where a stranger asks the question that reframes your next two years, where the collaboration that becomes a grant begins over bad coffee. A paper in the proceedings is a record of work. Presence is participation. The first is what I will have; the second is what was declined.

Nor is the loss only mine. My paper is co-authored with colleagues at a British university and an industrial partner, funded by a UK research council. British public money produced work that will be presented in Europe by someone other than the person who did it.

There is a version of this argument that ends in indignation. I would rather it ended in a proposal, because the fix here is unusually modest.

If a telephone interview can override a documented file, applicants should be told the call is coming. Not a hearing, not a right of appeal — simply notice, and a rough idea of what will be asked. It costs the consulate nothing. It would have cost me nothing. And it would have changed the outcome, because the difference between refusal and approval was not the strength of my case. It was whether I happened to be somewhere quiet, with my own paperwork to hand, when the phone rang.

Second: there is no published standard for what a sufficient answer looks like. How many places is enough? How much detail about a garden? Nobody says, and so an applicant cannot prepare, and an officer cannot be held to anything.

And third, when an officer records that justification "was not provided", it seems fair to ask whether the file in front of them contains it. Mine did. He was reading from it.

I did not choose the passport I hold. I have no part in the politics that make it inconvenient, and no means of resigning from them. What I have is the work.

So the paper will be presented, in a room I have never been in. The next one is already being written. I will keep publishing and keep submitting, and if the rooms remain closed I will send the research where I cannot go myself.

It won't stop me.
